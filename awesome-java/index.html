Close Reading #1

Richard III (1.3.43-54)

They do me wrong, and I will not endure it!

Who is it that complains unto the King

That I, forsooth, am stern and love them not?

By holy Paul, they love his Grace but lightly

That fill his ears with such dissentious rumors.

Because I cannot flatter and look fair,

Smile in men’s faces, smooth, deceive, and cog,

Duck with French nods and apish courtesy,

I must be held a rancorous enemy.

Cannot a plain man live and think no harm,

But thus his simple truth must be abused

With silken, sly, insinuating Jacks?

Few characters in Shakespeare’s plays rival Richard III as a master manipulator. From his very first speech, he deems himself as a villain and delights in this depiction. Yet his power does not lie only in his schemes, but also his words, he uses his ability to construct a convincing presentation that makes others believe the opposite of what he intends. A prime example comes in Richard III, Act 1, Scene 3, lines 43–54, when Richard defends himself against accusations of being harsh, dishonest, and deceitful. In this short speech, he protests that he is nothing more than a “plain man,” innocent of courtly scheming. To any attentive audience, the irony is overwhelming. The rhetoric he uses to claim simplicity betrays further calculation. In this passage, Richard constructs a convincing performance of genuine honesty that ironically reveals his calculated rhetorical skill and manipulative nature. Through calculated diction, strategic sound patterns, and self-contradicting imagery, Shakespeare allows the audience to see the  deception Richard denies, creating a speech that breaks down itself even as it is delivered. The literary devices and diction that undermine his claim to simplicity and the sonic dimension of the speech when performed aloud.

Richard’s self-presentation in this passage is strategically tailored to his direct audience while revealing his true nature to the viewing audience. At this point in the play, Richard is still Duke of Gloucester, though known from his opening speech that he has already begun turning against his family. His audience in the scene includes Queen Elizabeth and her faction. To them, Richard plays the part of the defamed victim, accused unfairly of being “stern” and “rancorous.” In Act 1, Scene 1, line 30, he has openly confessed to being “determined to prove a villain.” The dramatic irony is undeniable. His words conceal his true intention from the characters onstage meanwhile revealing to the audience offstage.

Richard intentionally presents himself as a wronged, honest man, professing resentment that his enemies spread “dissentious rumors.” He even feigns sarcasm with “forsooth” in line 45, mocking the very idea that he could be as “stern” as they claim. By naming the charge of being a “rancorous enemy” only to reject it, he appears forgiving while planting the idea that the accusations are baseless slanders. His goal is clear from the moment he starts talking. He wants  to deflect suspicion from himself by turning the accusations back on his opponents, portraying them as malicious schemers while he is the truth-teller. Having established Richard’s strategic position in context, it is easy to examine how the passage’s structure supports this performance.

The passage’s four-sentence structure moves deliberately from an outburst to a generalization, giving Richard’s personal defense the weight of universal truth. The first sentence bursts forth as an explosive declaration of injustice, “They do me wrong, and I will not endure it!” The resentment is extravagant. It is intended to catch attention and claim the moral high ground. The second sentence changes to a rhetorical question: “Who is it that complains unto the King”. Here Richard takes defense into offense, suggesting that only the blind could fail to notice how his enemies spread“dissentious” discord. His choice of word, dissentious, not only describes discord, but also positions the schemers as destructive to unity. The word, dissentious, according to the Oxford English Dictionary means, “Of, pertaining to, or characterized by, dissension or disagreement in opinion; esp. given to dissension, discordant, quarrelsome” (oed.com).

The main point of Richard’s defense lies in the building up of courtly vices: “flatter and look fair, / Smile in men’s faces, smooth, deceive, and cog, / Duck with French nods and apish courtesy” (lines 48–50). The list overwhelms with its sheer accumulation, presenting refined behavior as corruption. The choice of “cog,” a term that can mean “to employ fraud or deceit, to cheat”, expands Richard’s verbal register beyond formal to informal, showing his linguistic flexibility. Likewise, the word “apish” demeans politeness as “Ape-like in imitation; unreasonably imitative”, stripping nobility of their honor.

Richard divides the linguistic world into two categories being his own simpleness and his enemies’ deceit. His self-image relies on the blunt focus of being a “plain man,” who “live and think no harm”. In contrast, his enemies are disguised amongst the other word choices in his speech like “dissentious,” and “rancorous”. The contrast makes him appear sensible and honest while his enemies seem fake and overrefined. Shakespeare also plays with the concrete and abstract. Vice is embodied in physical gestures like “smile”, “smooth”, and “duck”. Richard takes on abstract virtue in “truth” and “plainness.” Even textile imagery reinforces this division as “smooth” and “silken” shows luxury and foreign beauty while Richard casts himself as plain English cloth. The racist remark at “French nods” appeals to anti-foreign prejudice, aligning Richard with national authenticity against imported corruption.

The very difficulty of performing these lines underscores the irony. The tongue-twisting pace of “flatter and look fair, / Smile in men’s faces, smooth, deceive, and cog” demands verbal agility, the opposite of unadorned plainness. The speech alternates between jagged, complex sound clusters and the artificially simple cadence of “Cannot a plain man live and think no harm?”—a contrast that feels rehearsed rather than spontaneous. Indeed, to say “smooth” requires smoothing the lips; to enact deception vocally requires artistry. The sonic demands of the speech prove that Richard’s supposed plain-spokenness is itself a carefully crafted performance.

This passage is a masterclass in dramatic irony. Richard demonstrates the very skills he denies possessing. His strategic positioning in context, his careful structuring of argument, his use of diction and devices, and the intricacy of his delivery all work together to create a speech that hides its own claim to simplicity. More broadly, this moment exemplifies Richard’s technique throughout the play. It is succeeding by performing honesty while practicing dishonesty, by claiming plainness while speaking with elaborate complexity. Shakespeare uses Richard to expose the deceitful danger of rhetoric itself. In a world where language can be “silken” and “insinuating,” truth and performance become interchangeable. Richard’s onstage audience cannot see the trick. Only the theater audience, who have been admitted into his confidence, notice the duplicity. Shakespeare implicates his audiences as witnesses to the very power and danger of rhetorical manipulation.
