Close Reading #3

For most of The Tempest, Caliban comes across as exactly what Prospero calls him, a monster, a slave, someone barely worth listening to. He curses, he plots murder, he is generally not having a great time. Then there is this one moment in Act 3, Scene 2, where he is talking to the two drunk idiots Stephano and Trinculo, and suddenly he gets weirdly poetic. He starts describing the island's sounds and his dreams, and the effect is kind of beautiful. In this passage, Shakespeare uses auditory imagery, dreamlike syntax, and layered metaphors to show a completely different side of Caliban, someone who is actually deeply connected to the island and its magic. The words he chooses, like "twangling," "dreaming," "riches," and "airs," do not just describe what he experiences. They actually shape how the audience understands both him and the island itself.

The first thing that stands out is how Caliban talks about sound. He says the island is "full of noises, / Sounds and sweet airs that give delight and hurt not" (3.2.149-150). The word "airs" is doing a lot of work here. According to the OED, it can mean both a musical melody and this ethereal, atmospheric quality, like the air itself is somehow special. So when Caliban uses this word, he is not just saying the island has nice music. He is suggesting that the entire atmosphere is magical, that one cannot really separate the physical environment from the enchantment. It is all one thing. The fact that these sounds "give delight and hurt not" is important too. For someone who has been enslaved and abused, the idea of something that only brings pleasure without pain seems almost unbelievable. The island, at least in this respect, offers him something Prospero never has. It gives him pure enjoyment without consequences.

Then Caliban gets more specific about what these sounds actually are. He says "Sometimes a thousand twangling instruments / Will hum about mine ears" (150-151). The word "twangling" apparently means this repeated vibrating sound, like from stringed instruments, but it can also suggest something slightly discordant or jangling. That is perfect for what Caliban is trying to describe. These are not just pretty harp sounds. They are overwhelming, they are everywhere ("a thousand"), and they have this quality that is both musical and maybe a little off. The word "hum" adds to this too. It is this constant, surrounding sound. The sense is that the island is almost alive with music, that it is impossible to escape but also impossible to fully pin down. The slight dissonance in "twangling" also mirrors Caliban's own position in the play. He is caught between different worlds, neither fully monster nor fully human, neither completely brutal nor completely sensitive.

What really stands out about this speech is how Caliban moves from talking about sounds to talking about dreams. He says "and sometimes voices / That, if I then had waked after long sleep, / Will make me sleep again; and then, in dreaming, / The clouds methought would open" (151-154). The syntax here gets all fluid and confusing, which actually works perfectly. He is describing this state where sleeping and waking blur together, where sounds lead to sleep which leads to dreams. The word "dreaming" marks the shift from what is actually happening to what is in his head. Shakespeare makes it hard to tell where one ends and the other begins though. It begs the question: is Caliban hearing real magical sounds, or is he dreaming? Does it matter? For someone in his position, enslaved, powerless, stuck on this island, his imagination might be the only space where he has any freedom. The dream world becomes as real and important as the waking one, maybe more so.

In these dreams, something amazing happens. The clouds open up and "show riches / Ready to drop upon me, that when I waked / I cried to dream again" (155-157). The word "riches" can mean actual material wealth, but it can also mean abundance or bounty in a more figurative sense. For Caliban, it probably means both. He is literally poor and enslaved, so the idea of riches dropping on him has obvious appeal. But it seems like more than that. These riches represent everything he does not have. Freedom, beauty, comfort, maybe even respect. They are "ready to drop," just out of reach, which is almost crueler than not having them at all. He can see what he wants, but only in dreams. The final line drives this home. "When I waked / I cried to dream again." His waking life is so bad that he cries when he has to return to it. The only place he can experience richness, in any sense, is in sleep. Reality offers him nothing.

What makes this passage so effective is how it completely changes the understanding of Caliban. Up until this point, he has been mostly defined by his anger and his crudeness. But here, Shakespeare shows that he is also imaginative, sensitive, and deeply attuned to beauty. The language he uses is sophisticated and layered. He does not just say "the island has nice sounds." He creates this whole multi-sensory experience that moves from hearing to dreaming to seeing. The auditory imagery of "airs" and "twangling" combines with the visual imagery of clouds opening and riches dropping. It all works together to create this sense of the island as a magical, abundant, but also frustrating place. Caliban can perceive all this beauty and possibility, but he cannot actually have it. His sensitivity almost makes his situation worse, because he is aware of everything he is missing.

This matters because Shakespeare is showing how language shapes perception, especially for characters who are marginalized or oppressed. Caliban's mastery of poetic language gives him the ability to articulate his experience of the island in ways that even Prospero does not. Prospero sees the island as his domain to control. Caliban experiences it as something alive, magical, and beyond human control. His language mediates between the physical world and his inner imaginative life, creating meaning where others might only see a "monster." The fact that he can express himself so beautifully makes his enslavement even more unjust, and that seems to be Shakespeare's point.

Looking at this passage closely, what strikes the reader most is how Shakespeare uses specific word choices to create layers of meaning. "Airs," "twangling," "dreaming," "riches." Each of these words does more than one thing, carrying both literal and figurative weight. They show that Caliban is capable of complex thought and feeling, that he engages with his environment in sophisticated ways, and that his humanity is far more developed than other characters recognize. Even minor or supposedly "monstrous" characters in Shakespeare have these moments of unexpected depth. That is part of what makes his plays still worth reading, probably. He does not let anyone be simple or one-dimensional. Even the slave who has been called a monster gets a speech that is more poetic than most of what the nobles say. And that feels deliberate, like Shakespeare is asking the audience to reconsider who actually has value and who gets to speak beautifully about their experience. Caliban might be powerless, but his language gives him a kind of authority that no one can completely take away.
